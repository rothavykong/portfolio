BTA Sports
Spring 2026

The 30 → 700 Experiment

How I A/B tested my way to 20× content growth in two weeks.

The Problem

BTA Sports' TikTok content was stuck. The account was producing well-shot videos that reflected the brand, but the average post was getting 30 views. The marketing team's hypothesis was "the algorithm is broken." Mine was different: the algorithm was working — it was just telling us our hooks were bad.

The Approach

Most "social media analytics" work in college is descriptive: pull a CTR report, draw conclusions. I treated this as an experimentation problem instead. I built a hook taxonomy (question hooks, statement hooks, visual hooks, controversy hooks), held everything else constant — same product, same length, same poster, same time-of-day window — and ran iterative posts as A/B tests over two weeks. After each post I logged hook type, view count at 24h and 72h, and audience retention curve from TikTok Studio in a small Excel tracker.

What I Found

Two patterns showed up. First, question hooks that named a specific player by first name out-performed every other category by roughly 4–6×. Second, the first 1.5 seconds mattered more than the next 13 — retention curves were nearly identical after the hook landed. This meant we weren't producing bad videos; we were producing videos with weak openings.

"The algorithm wasn't broken. The first 1.5 seconds were."

Why It Mattered

The next post — built deliberately around the winning hook structure — went from the 30-view baseline to 700+ views overnight. A 20× lift in two weeks, with no change in production budget or posting cadence. More importantly, we now had a repeatable rule, not a lucky post. I rolled the findings into a Power BI dashboard so the team could keep the experiment running after my hours ended; reporting time dropped roughly 40% because the team stopped pulling numbers manually.

What I'd Do Differently

I'd structure the test more rigorously from day one. I was running roughly one post per day with no formal control, which means I'm citing 20× with a small sample. With more time I'd pre-register hook hypotheses, post matched pairs in the same 24-hour window, and bootstrap confidence intervals on the retention curves. The lift is real; the precision could be better.

Centre College
Spring 2026

Reading 100,000 Strangers

What 100K+ user journeys revealed about how students actually choose a college.

The Problem

Centre College's Marketing & Strategic Communications team runs the "Go & Do" recruitment campaign — a major piece of the funnel that turns visitors into applicants. They had a Google Analytics 4 property collecting behavior from 100K+ users on the campaign landing pages, and the same question every marketing team eventually asks: which parts of this site actually move the needle, and which are decoration?

The Approach

I started by resisting the temptation to build a dashboard. Dashboards answer the question "what happened?" — but the team needed to know "what should we change?", which is a different question. So I spent the first week mapping the user journey by hand: from first landing-page hit, through scroll depth, internal navigation, time-on-page, to the events that mattered (apply-now clicks, info-request submissions). Only after I had a hypothesis-rich map did I open Power BI.

"Dashboards answer 'what happened?' The team needed to know 'what should we change?'"

What I'm Finding

Live project — case study writeup in progress. Early signals suggest scroll depth on the campaign landing page plateaus before users reach the primary CTA, and that users who navigate to "student life" within their first 90 seconds behave very differently from those who don't. Final findings and the recommendation memo will land here when the project ships.

What I'd Do Differently

GA4 alone can't answer "why" — it can only answer "what." If I were starting over I'd pair the behavioral data with a small set of qualitative interviews (5–7 prospective students) to triangulate the quantitative patterns. I'd also build the experimentation infrastructure earlier, so the team could test the recommended changes rather than just implementing them.

Open Dev Cambodia
Winter 2026

The Pipeline That Replaced 80% of Manual Work

Scraping, cleaning, and standardizing 2,000+ business records.

The Problem

Open Development Cambodia produces research on labor incidents and economic development — work that depends on broad, well-structured datasets about Cambodian businesses. The existing process was manual: an analyst would visit business directory websites and copy-paste relevant fields into a spreadsheet. The volume the team needed (low thousands of records) was bottlenecked by the time it took to gather them. There was no analysis pipeline because there was no clean data pipeline.

The Approach

I built the missing layer. I scoped the target sites, mapped the DOM structure of each, and used Playwright (over BeautifulSoup, because the target sites loaded content dynamically via JavaScript and BS4 couldn't see half the data) to scrape ~2,000 business records. Then I treated the cleaning step as a real engineering problem rather than a one-off: a Pandas + Power Query workflow that normalized address formats, deduplicated near-matches, validated phone numbers, and flagged records that needed human review.

What I Found

The interesting finding was process, not data. Roughly 30% of records in the existing manual dataset had at least one normalization error (inconsistent province names, duplicate entries from name variants, malformed phone numbers). The pipeline didn't just save time — it improved the accuracy of every analysis downstream of it.

"Manual data collection isn't slow because humans are slow. It's slow because humans are inconsistent."

Why It Mattered

Manual collection time dropped roughly 80%. More importantly, the team can now ask questions of a clean dataset that they couldn't ask of the messy one — questions about geographic distribution, sector concentration, and labor incident clustering that depend on records being comparable.

What I'd Do Differently

I'd build the pipeline as a scheduled job from day one rather than a one-time script. Business directories change; a snapshot from January 2026 is already partially stale. A weekly cron with diff-based change detection would keep the dataset alive without ongoing manual work.

Coursework
Spring 2026

The $1.2 Billion Question

Building a default-prediction model and asking what it's actually worth.

The Problem

Most credit-risk modeling exercises end at the AUC score. A team builds a model, reports a ROC-AUC, and claims success. But a 0.75 AUC isn't a business outcome — it's an input to one. The harder question, and the one a lender actually pays you to answer, is: "If we deployed this, how much money would it make or save us?" That's the question I wanted to answer.

The Approach

The technical work was logistic regression on 307K+ applicants and 1.4M+ records (debt utilization, payment delays, demographic features), with cross-validation and confusion-matrix evaluation — standard. The interesting work was after the model was built. I translated the confusion matrix into expected dollar outcomes by attaching realistic loan amounts and loss-given-default assumptions to each cell — true positives became "loans correctly declined → losses prevented," false negatives became "missed bad loans → losses incurred," false positives became "good loans incorrectly declined → revenue forgone." I scored a 61K-row test set through this lens.

What I Found

At a 0.81 recall and 0.75 ROC-AUC, the model would prevent an estimated $1.2B in loan losses on the test set under reasonable assumptions about average loan size and default cost. The strongest predictors were debt-to-income ratio and recent payment-delay history — not surprising, but quantifying their relative weight changed how I'd recommend the model be used: not as an autonomous decline engine but as a triage layer feeding into human underwriting for borderline cases.

"The loss function the model is trained on is rarely the loss function the business cares about."

Why It Mattered

Most student credit-risk projects I've seen stop at the AUC. The dollar translation is the part a credit officer actually cares about. The exercise taught me something I now apply to every model: the loss function the model is trained on is rarely the loss function the business cares about, and translating between the two is most of the job.

What I'd Do Differently

Logistic regression was the right starting point for interpretability but probably leaves performance on the table. I'd benchmark gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) and use SHAP for feature importance to keep interpretability. I'd also build a calibration plot — a model that's accurate on average but miscalibrated at the tails will misprice risk in exactly the cases that matter most.
